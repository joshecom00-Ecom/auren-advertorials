Phil Cleary spent 34 years swinging hammers, framing walls, and building houses with his bare hands.

He wasn't just a carpenter. He was the guy neighbours called when their deck was rotting, and the one his friends trusted to help renovate their kitchens on weekends. His hands weren't just tools — they were his identity.

So when arthritis started creeping into his fingers at 54, he ignored it.

"Just wear and tear," his doctor told him. "Part of getting older."

Phil believed him. He pushed through the stiffness. Gripped his tools tighter. Popped ibuprofen like mints.

But by 57, the pain had spread from his fingers to his wrists, his elbows, and both knees.

He couldn't close his fist around a hammer handle.

The same hands that had built over 200 homes couldn't open a jar of coffee.

At 58, Phil was medically retired. Not by choice. His doctor signed the papers and told him to "take it easy."

"Take it easy? I don't know how to take it easy. That's not who I am."

What followed was 18 months of the kind of frustration — and a small fortune poured down the drain — that anyone with arthritis knows too well.

His doctor started him on anti-inflammatory pills. Ibuprofen, three times a day. At arthritis doses — 1,200mg or more daily — he was burning through a bottle every two weeks. $12 here. $20 there. The pills dulled the edge for a few hours, then wore off by dinner. So he'd take more. And more. At the end of 12 months, Phil had spent over $300 on pills alone — and his joints were worse than when he started.

Then came the creams.

Voltaren. Tiger Balm. Deep Heat. His bathroom cabinet looked like a pharmacy shelf. A single tube of Voltaren runs $26 to $34 — and at four applications a day, it barely lasts three weeks. Phil was going through a tube and a half every month. That's over $500 a year — on a cream that stops working the moment it dries.

"I'd rub it on at 7am. By 9am it was like I'd never put it on."

His wife suggested glucosamine supplements. $25 to $40 a month for the triple-strength stuff. He took them religiously for four months — nearly $150 spent — and felt absolutely nothing. Large studies have since shown that glucosamine provides, at best, modest benefit for osteoarthritis. Phil could have told them that for free.

Then his doctor recommended cortisone injections.

"This'll sort you out," the doctor said.

The first injection cost $200 out of pocket. It worked — for about six weeks. The second one was $250. It lasted barely three. By the third — another $200 — Phil could hardly tell he'd had it.

Three injections. Nearly $700. Less than four months of declining relief.

And that's just one joint. Phil's arthritis was in his fingers, his wrists, his elbows, and both knees. His doctor told him he could get three to four injections per joint, per year. Phil did the maths in his head. Even at two joints, that's $1,500 to $2,000 a year — for a treatment that wears off faster every time.

Each appointment meant driving 40 minutes, sitting in a waiting room for 45 minutes, and seeing a doctor for five.

Then there were the compression gloves — $35. The paraffin wax bath — $80. The copper-infused sleeve from a late-night TV ad — $45. Each one used twice, then stuffed in a drawer.

When Phil sat down and added it all up, he'd spent over $3,000 in 18 months on treatments that gave him nothing but a thinner wallet and a bathroom cabinet full of empty promises.

And through all of it, one thing stayed the same…

Every single one of those companies kept getting paid.
